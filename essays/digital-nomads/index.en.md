# Digital Nomads: How to Work While Traveling the World

*Geng Yue · Travel Guides*

Today, let's talk about a "digital nomad."

**"As long as I have a laptop and the internet, I can work from anywhere in the world."**

While you — yes, you — come home too exhausted to speak after your nine-to-five, this high school dropout has the audacity to say something like that.

His name is Jacob Laukaitis. Over the past two years, carrying nothing but a laptop and an internet connection, he has lived and worked in 30 countries, never staying anywhere longer than a month. The moment he grows tired of a place, he buys a plane ticket and flies to the next destination.

He's not a bohemian, not a backpacker, and certainly no trust fund kid. He doesn't leech off his parents, either. He is a "digital nomad," an entrepreneur — **"wandering the world" is both his work and his way of life.**

![Jacob Laukaitis](images/002.jpg)

He was born in Lithuania, Eastern Europe. He started his first company at 15 and sold it at 16. At 17, after finishing high school, he set out to see the world, traveling and working simultaneously. He spent a month in Japan, then earned a deep-sea diving license in a village in the Philippines, swam with whale sharks, climbed volcanoes in Bali, explored hundreds of temples in Myanmar and Cambodia, rode a motorcycle 8,000 kilometers in four weeks across the Balkan Peninsula, and passed through countless islands, villages, and cities.

![Jacob traveling](images/003.jpg)

During this time, he participated in several startup projects. The internet company he founded in May 2014, which helps thrifty Americans find shopping coupons, now has 20 full-time employees and is steadily profitable.

For Jacob, the greatest reward of the "digital nomad" lifestyle is the ability to rapidly absorb the essence of different countries and cultures. He has spent considerable time studying the histories of various continents, different cultures, and online business opportunities. He says he is growing at an unprecedented pace and meeting fascinating people from all over. Although he is always on the road alone, he never feels lonely. He always befriends locals and quickly integrates into their lives.

How does he do it?

**▼"My parents' generation had a very simple view of 'success' — find a job, and do it for the rest of your life."**

In his view, this means "staying comfortably in your comfort zone," living a "simple, predictable" life: show up at the office at 9, make sure you look busy all day, leave at 5.

![Nine-to-five animation](images/004.gif)

As a "digital nomad," **you can "design" your life.**

The free-spirited version goes like this: if you love surfing, you can move to California; if you love cycling, you can spend two months riding around Taiwan; if you love motorcycle road trips, you can spend six weeks on a long ride through Vietnam. If you love playing mahjong, then Chengdu welcomes you. If you want to climb the Himalayas, vacation in Hawaii, or eat barbecue — go for it. If you need to attend your mother's birthday party or a friend's graduation today, then go. You can do whatever you like. As long as you deliver great work and produce excellent results, who cares where you work from?

![Design your life](images/005.jpg)

**What is it like when you are liberated from both physical location and time constraints?**

First, you feel amazing. You get to choose where you live and how you work.

Jacob's actual work schedule involves 3-5 hours of work per day. In his first year, he visited 12 countries. "I don't set fixed plans for myself, but I know where I'm going and what I'm doing. Starting on the 1st of next month, I'll spend a week resting in Thailand, then a month in Bali having great exchanges with my colleagues, another month traveling around Indonesia learning the local language, two months traveling in India (including two weeks living in a Mumbai slum). Three months in Taiwan, learning Mandarin and connecting with local startups and communities. Three months on a long trip through China, visiting potential destinations."

![Free travel](images/006.jpg)

The flip side of boundless freedom is boundless responsibility. You feel unprecedented pressure. Because you must design your own work style and find a method that suits you and is efficient. You are the chief architect of your own career, the captain, the pilot, the conductor.

Those employees who love flying around and are always ready to switch into fun mode are not necessarily the "worst, most out-of-control" workers. As a boss, Jacob believes that people are most efficient when they are in environments they enjoy and feel comfortable in, when their spirits are highest. Because they are willing to invest time in things they are passionate about — the more they love their work, the more proactive they become, the more attentive they are to users, and ultimately the more revenue and sales they generate for the company.

![Efficient work](images/007.jpg)

▼

**Pursuit**

"It's easy to look busy in an office — attend meetings, reply to emails, grab coffee... We've all been there, haven't we? But only when you work remotely, when every minute is your own and under your own control, do you repeatedly ask yourself: how can I produce accurate results efficiently? Besides, if we only hire in the San Francisco area, we'd miss out on talent from all over the world. Now, we can hire talent from anywhere, and save on office rent."

Jacob doesn't envy many of his peers' pursuit of material wealth and comfortable living. He believes society is overly fixated on material things, obsessed with "possession." As Dave Ramsay said, "People buy things they don't need with money they don't have to impress people they don't like."

Jacob believes another constraint of caring too much about **material wealth** is: **you have to maintain all those possessions, and they tie you firmly to one place, making you immobile.** At least for him, on the first night he packs all his belongings into a backpack, and by the next morning, with a plane ticket in hand, he's already on his way.

![Traveling light](images/008.jpg)

▼

**No "Right Time"**

Some words from Steve Jobs strengthened his inner voice: "Remembering that you are going to die is the best way I know to avoid the trap of thinking you have something to lose. **You are already naked. There is no reason not to follow your heart.**"

Jacob says, if you want to explore this world, then do it now. It doesn't require sacrificing your career. There are thousands of "us" ready to help you, countless ways to make a living, and infinite experiences waiting for you — and distant horizons to see.

In this world, there are countless ways to live.

This is one that I envy,

Reminded of Peter Hessler, author of River Town, and his final advice to young writers:

"Leave home. Leave your family, leave your familiar routine, leave your comfortable social circle. Spend time with people who are different from you."

May you soon live the life you most want to live.
