# How Do People Who Travel All Year Round Make a Living?

*Geng Yue · Travel Guides*

![Opening animation](images/002.gif)

**Life is like a story: what matters is not how long it is, but how splendid. As is a tale, so is life: not how long it is, but how good it is, is what matters.**

![Traveler silhouette](images/003.jpg)

Every time you want to start something, you feel you're never quite ready. You might recall that line from Ang Lee's *Eat Drink Man Woman*: "Life is not like cooking—you can't have all the ingredients ready before you put them in the pot."

For those who love travel, a persistent question arises: how can you set out frequently, or stay on the road for a long time? Where does the money come from? How do you find so much time?

It's easy to envy those globetrotters when they're having the time of their lives on the road, but you must also believe: they've made vast preparations behind the scenes. Here are stories and advice from some who've lived it...

**@沈浩翔**

Since setting out last July, **we've passed through:** Hong Kong, Macau, Chiang Mai, the Philippines (Manila, Boracay), Singapore, Malaysia (Penang, KL, Malacca), Australia (Perth, Western Australia road trip, Gold Coast, Brisbane, Melbourne, Great Ocean Road self-drive), **then entered New Zealand to begin working-holiday travel.**

![Travel route](images/004.jpg)

**Our route around New Zealand:** Auckland, Whangarei, other spots in the northern North Island, Tauranga, Rotorua, Taupo, Napier, Hastings, Waipukurau, the village with the world's longest name, Wellington, Picton, villages in the Nelson area, Westport, Karamea, Greymouth, Fox Glacier, Wanaka, Arrowtown, Queenstown, Lake Tekapo, Timaru, Oamaru, Dunedin, Christchurch.

**Our finances are roughly balanced at this point.**

First, understand that travel is not tourism. Travel is long-term; to survive long-term, you can't live the way tourists do—so don't expect to eat and drink well every day. If someone is doing long-term travel in comfort and style, they either have deep pockets or substantial sponsorships.

**◇ Super Money-Saving Methods — the tried-and-true for working-holiday travelers**

![Budget travel](images/005.jpg)

**Southeast Asia:** costs are low, and budget airlines plus budget hotels (like AirAsia's Tune Hotel) work just fine. For food, just follow the street stalls—no planning needed.

**Traveling in developed countries** means bringing your own utensils, pots and pans, rice, oil, and salt. If you eat out three meals a day, you won't last long. Most YHA (Youth Hostels) and BBH (New Zealand/Australia-only backpacker hostels) have kitchens.

![Hostel kitchen](images/006.jpg)

But beds at 25+ dollars per night per person still aren't sustainable long-term. If you're driving, you'll often sleep in the car. Without a car, you'll sometimes pitch a tent. Cooking three meals a day yourself, you can keep a week's food budget around 70 dollars. Settling in one city, you can rent—weekly expenses around 150 dollars, with a mobile plan at 20–30 dollars a month. That's the baseline. Staying put, weekly petrol runs about 70 dollars; on long drives, fuel consumption is roughly 7.2L per 100 km, at about 2.15 dollars per liter, varying by region.

![Self-drive travel](images/007.jpg)

**New Zealand and Australia suit self-driving. Europe suits rail travel,** with various rail passes available. In NZ/Aus there are $1 car rentals—helping relocate vehicles to their origin, you just have to reach a destination within a set time. Nude Bus (Naked Bus) often runs $1 ticket promotions.

Moreover, **if you really want to save,** you can couchsurf or do work exchange along the way (trading 2–4 hours of labor for room and board), WWOOF (World Wide Opportunities on Organic Farms), and hitchhike. In New Zealand, hitchhiking is easy—usually 5–30 minutes to catch a ride. **Except on the desolate South Island West Coast route.** Apply the same approach to Europe and it won't cost much either.

On this basis, as a couple in NZ/Aus, settling in one city costs about 11,000 RMB per month. Ironically, traveling around costs only 7,000–8,000 RMB. In Southeast Asia, 4,000–5,000 RMB a month suffices.

**◇ Now for the income question everyone cares about**

![Working income](images/008.jpg)

**1. Working! The most direct approach!** (Only a Working Holiday Visa lets you work legally and pay taxes. Otherwise it's under-the-table work with zero protection.) If you want to do what others can't, you must endure what others won't. That's the simplest principle of equivalent exchange. Work as a traveler and you'll see the底层 of this world up close—because travelers mostly do the hardest, most grueling jobs, not so different from undocumented immigrants.

I worked as a laborer at a Chinese supermarket in Auckland, New Zealand, for three months. A legendary laborer there once said: **"You want the boss's money; the boss wants your life!"** I worked 12 hours a day, barely stopping even to eat, six days a week. A coworker named Xiao Wang hadn't had a day off in the six months before I arrived.

During those three months, my wife worked sorting at a vegetable factory—her longest day was 14 hours, averaging about 12 daily. Other working-holiday travelers I asked reported similar hours: 11–12 per day.

![Working life](images/009.jpg)

**Here's what my days looked like:** up at 6:10, brew two cups of coffee, eat breakfast, leave at 6:40, drop my wife at work by 7:00, reach the supermarket at 7:20, glance at the book manuscript I was translating, start work at 7:30.

Move all produce from the cold room outside—around 9:30. Then check which shelves need restocking and start replenishing. **Best case: by 11:30 you get a 30-minute window to eat lunch; worst case: no stopping.** Deliveries come between 12:30 and 14:30—unload immediately into storage, about 7–12 pallets, 20 crates each, averaging 18 kg per crate.

After stocking, restock shelves again. Spot spoiled produce and remove it—if no female worker is handling spoilage in the back, you have to deal with it yourself. At 4:30, start putting out the next day's stock; if you're lucky, finish by 5:30. Half an hour of cleanup, then at 6:00 start packing up—unload all produce into crates and into the cold room. Best case: done by 7:00; worst case: 7:30.

![Supermarket work](images/010.jpg)

If my wife hadn't finished, I'd rush home to eat, then start cooking. If she was off, I'd go pick her up.

She'd be dead on her feet; I'd finish cooking, and by 9:30 we could collapse into bed—no energy left even to shower. Sometimes she'd get off early and cook instead.

I tried adding two hours of translation after cooking for about a week—it broke me; I gave up.

**That was the purgatory of the working phase.** I could eat a kilogram of rice a day and still lost 20 jin (~10 kg).

![Behind the supermarket](images/011.jpg)

**Behind the supermarket were three large dumpsters. They revealed every shade of humanity.**

Impoverished Maori, Pacific Islanders, and white people would pick through the dumpsters for wilted vegetables—maybe to feed chickens at home, maybe to eat themselves. Nearby restaurants would come take our discarded cardboard boxes—yes, the boxes we threw out, they used to pack takeout for their customers.

Now we're in Christchurch working a second job: opening green-lipped mussels. Work from 6 AM to 2 PM, 15 minutes' rest every 2 hours, hands never stopping. 500 mussels opened earns 7.8 dollars; during training, it's hourly pay. After training, piece-rate kicks in. After a week, we could open about 750 per hour—approaching 900, which beats the hourly rate. **Day one of mussel opening: my wife's hands hurt so much she couldn't sleep and cried. And mussel opening is considered one of the easier working-holiday jobs.** Worse are fruit-picking and farm work. Winter pruning is pure purgatory. The hardest of all are the jobs that destroy your back.

Easiest is cashier work—easier to find in small towns; next is kitchen hand (they usually don't hire women); then cleaning work.

I also did carpentry. **Construction pays well if you're good, but there are basically no contracts—wage theft is common.** My first gypsum-board job: the boss owed others 30,000 dollars and was speculating on real estate. After my first week, I tearfully told him I couldn't even pay rent. On a rainy night at 11 PM, I successfully demanded my wages—then bolted.

![Budget travel](images/005.jpg)

**2. Translation**

During my travels I helped proofread a book that was published. I earn royalties based on sales, but since I only proofread, the income is negligible. And **in New Zealand and Australia, where even cafés rarely have internet, cross-border collaboration is a torment.**

My first round of proofreading was done in fits and starts at an Australian airport. The main translator handled most of the later work, so I was too embarrassed to claim much compensation.

During the final push, when the project team disagreed, I was doing a work exchange at Fox Glacier—with only 200 MB of data for two weeks. I used every byte, mainly sending and receiving emails, forcefully setting the project workflow, even overriding my own teacher without saving face.

![Translation work](images/012.jpg)

The second book I translated while working in Auckland. Working and translating at the same time basically made me mentally impaired. Later, after quitting, I spent two solid weeks translating. **While traveling, whenever I could get online at a library, I'd communicate with the publisher, always trying to finish a week early to avoid delays when I'd next be offline. But translation is unreliable:** the going rate is 50–70 RMB per thousand characters. A published book might earn 4,000–6,000 RMB, but you'll basically spend a month on it. The return on investment is low. And my own royalties are still a long way off—if I waited for them, I'd starve.

![Translation scene](images/013.jpg)

**3. Writing**

I'm writing a book, but purely for interest. **First, there's no guarantee it'll be published. It won't feed you. Second,** what I've written above might have some substance for people who haven't traveled this way, but for fellow travelers, it's old news—chewing wax.

Books like *Backpack Decade* or *Working Holiday* are basically written for homebodies who dream of travel but almost certainly will never go. Including newer titles—they're all the same.

**Third, those books had timing on their side—they came out early.** The early wave profited off armchair dreamers; latecomers have virtually no chance of getting published. This kind of content only needs to appear once. So either you have money and can write about experiences no one else has covered (any backpacker can write about budget travel—it's been done to death), or you avoid the over-traveled regions like Southeast Asia or NZ/Aus. Forget Lijiang and Tibet. Unless you go somewhere like Afghanistan or Lebanon and come back with stories—*that's* the way.

**Fourth, writing requires regular time and a reliable internet connection.** Fragmented travel time always lacks something—sometimes no power, sometimes no internet. When both are available, your partner says "let's go shopping" or "let's watch a terrible movie," and there goes your quiet writing time. Get online, start answering questions, and the manuscript dies again. Honestly, it's my own lack of self-discipline.

**Forget about making money from writing.**

![Writing](images/014.jpg)

**4. Existing Assets**

Renting out our home brings in a little each month.

**Having your own company** helps. I get about 1,000 RMB a month from domestic advertising—better than nothing. Since I'm not managing it, I don't expect growth.

My friend who went to Pakistan saved an extra 30,000 RMB over 10 months of travel because he has his own company. On the road, you meet all kinds; occasionally helping someone with accounting earns a bit. Selling his company's medical devices on the side also makes money. As he puts it, this kills the joy of travel. Also, he told me not to copy him—"earn certain kinds of money and sooner or later you'll end up inside."

I know some early travelers whose finances entered a positive cycle—riding the wave of China's social transformation. Someone bought property in Shanghai in 2000 and sold in 2011—boom, they were set. Another hiked for 9 days through the primal forest of Daming Mountain, opened a trekking route, then started a youth hostel—being early, they struck gold. Later they'd buy an island in Qiandao Lake or a mountain somewhere. Money was no longer a problem.

![Property investment](images/015.jpg)

**5. Daigou (Personal Shopping)**

I didn't do daigou. I have the traffic and could source products—it's convenient. But it's tedious; I don't want to do customer service, run out to stock up every day, or manage an online shop. Neither does my wife.

**Earn this money and you can forget about a carefree travel experience.**

Real daigou practitioners close their online shop for a stretch, travel purely, then buy a pile of stuff, return home, and furiously run the shop for another stretch. It keeps you perpetually on the hook.

And every purchase requires capital upfront—this is an investment with uncertain payback, not suited for the cash-strapped, but for those who don't mind the outlay.

Answering a daily barrage of newbie questions is maddening.

The margins will inevitably be squeezed to nothing, until everything ships from Yiwu. **It's not a long-term play—no point wasting your life on something so ephemeral.**

![Daigou](images/016.jpg)

**6. Design? Freelance?**

**Forget it. Without fixed time and a stable internet connection, you can't pull this off.** Negotiating a project takes an unknown amount of time; meanwhile, you're fronting rent, internet, and living expenses. For a traveler who moves on a whim, it's wildly unreliable.

Through business connections, I'd already met design firms in New Zealand, the largest Chinese-language media company, and the CEO of a social media company. We discussed potential collaborations, but since I had no fixed address, most fizzled out.

Why would I do this kind of work abroad? If it's just to earn money, I'd rather do the most grueling manual labor than transplant my domestic survival model.

**Doing what you've never done is the meaningful experience; doing what you're used to is meaningless. Travel is meant to find personal breakthroughs—not to go abroad only to discover you prefer comfort, and retreat into familiar habits, crawling back into your shell.**

To break through, be brave. To be brave, prepare for the worst. Having prepared for the worst, you're ready to set aside face and dignity, endure the bitterest hardship, and repeat the simplest tasks countless times until you do them a little better than before.

![Breakthrough](images/017.jpg)

**7. Stock Library Photographer**

This is **relatively reliable,** but you need good equipment and to understand stock-photo requirements. While traveling, you must be patient about capturing good photos, and it helps if you can find friends willing to lend a studio.

Before departing, I bought a book called *Turn Your Photos into Money*, but I never got around to implementing it. First, registering accounts is a hassle; second, royalty payouts are extremely slow; third, I couldn't afford a decent camera—just a 500D with an entry-level lens.

![Photography equipment](images/018.jpg)

**8. Securing Sponsorships**

First, you need to know how to shoot video (most people can), then how to edit. You have to produce a polished piece before you can approach TV networks about collaboration. After that, you need luck.

Directing shots and then doing all the post-production yourself takes enormous time. Your partner needs to be camera-ready and act for the lens—something most people can't do.

Though I can edit and my wife is good on camera, I've never tried this approach.

In summary, act early: opening hostels, writing books, working-holiday travel, daigou—these are all played out, unprofitable, and won't change you. If you truly want travel to transform you, throw yourself into something no guidebook has covered, something no one else has done. That's where a sliver of possibility remains to escape your mundane life. If you can't bring yourself to attempt uncertain ventures, if you must pre-analyze every question or consult predecessors before starting anything—my advice: don't just drop the idea of "safe travel"; honestly, entrepreneurship isn't for you either. Be content, be ordinary, live out your life in peace. Idealism will kill you.

![Summary](images/019.jpg)

**Someone asks: if it sounds so miserable, why travel at all? I never said I was traveling for pleasure.**

My goal has always been: make nothing for three years, then make three years' income in one stroke. Working for a stretch, then traveling for a stretch, is one experiment toward that aim. And before setting out, I'd been making all kinds of such experiments.

Truthfully, a gap year for me is just an extra-long vacation. Working-holiday travel is merely an excuse for spending that vacation. At least it's a relatively normal way to spend a year. If I told people I wanted to experiment with making nothing for a year, earning two years' provisions in one, then working one year and resting one—the cost of explaining to family and friends would be too high, and the number of people wanting to strangle me would increase dramatically.

Also, many who know me know I can't accept an ordinary life; but even more friends who've traveled with me know I'm not a wild character. My travel state is just middling, methodical. Neither deeply unhinged nor comfortably bourgeois, neither spiritual retreat nor genuine austerity—a half-measure, really. Calling it "uneven" is just putting it nicely.

## Other Answers

![Other perspectives](images/020.jpg)

**@kaka2008:** I won't make sweeping claims—just two examples I know of.

1. Someone who's already earned enough that interest plus investments can support a full year of travel. For instance, a Weibo big-V with 830,000 followers is a software entrepreneur and admits to strong finances. From their posts, they frequently travel the world—last year they went to Africa and Antarctica. One post indicated a solo trip to South Africa cost around 100,000+ RMB.

2. Someone who lives by writing travelogues and reports for newspapers. For example, the American writer Peter Hessler lived in China for years—first teaching in Fuling for two years, then settling in Beijing as a Wall Street Journal correspondent, often driving across China. He wrote for several American newspapers, funding his travels with freelance fees. Later, he turned his experiences into two books—*Country Driving* and *Oracle Bones*.

**Most people, I think, have some means of livelihood.** Those who quit their jobs to travel are the minority. After all, **if you can't feed yourself, you can't keep traveling.**

![Making a living](images/021.jpg)

**@Jarod Zhang:** I believe the best solution for sustainable travel is to **become a digital nomad.** I previously wrote an article about this lifestyle; I'll reproduce it here in hopes it's useful. The term "digital nomad" translates from the English "Digital Nomad." Who coined it is untraceable, but with the development of internet technology, it has moved from an obscure niche concept into mainstream media and is becoming an increasingly popular lifestyle.

So what is the digital nomad lifestyle? Let me put it in plain Chinese: **Simply put, it's using income from internet-based freelance work or entrepreneurship to support location-independent living and travel. More simply: work and play at the same time—travel and earn, neither neglected.**

The digital nomad lifestyle is the mortal enemy of the nine-to-five. It rejects office cubicles, rejects commuting, rejects overtime, and especially rejects the 10-day (or fewer) crumb of paid annual leave. Digital nomad entrepreneurship differs from traditional entrepreneurship: it doesn't require large initial capital or the ability to pitch VCs with a PowerPoint—it demands your time and passion. The digital nomad lifestyle says: go where you want, do what you want!

![Digital nomads](images/022.jpg)

**@aloha:** I found a travel method on Quora: through **World Wide Opportunities on Organic Farms**, you work on organic farms in the host country in exchange for free room and board. Interested? Check out wwoof.org.

**@minyuan:** Let me share my own experience. After graduating from university last year, I traveled solo for six months—Yunnan, Tibet, Nepal, India, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand. I met many long-term travelers on the road; most were traveling on savings (the majority had quit their jobs), with a tiny handful supported by family.

Travelers' income sources are essentially twofold: **1. Previous savings.** This goes without saying—most people fall into this category.

**2. Earning while traveling**—which can be subdivided by whether the work is travel-related or not. Travel-related: writers (filing articles for newspapers and magazines, publishing books), photographers (usually also writers), professional travelers (typically having one of those two identities), travel hustlers (street vending, busking, daigou). Not travel-related: you could be a software developer—write code from anywhere. **You can be in any profession, as long as you're free enough and can earn enough.**

![Earning on the road](images/023.jpg)

**@陈宇欣:** The globetrotters I know all have solid jobs in real life—working hard, saving hard, hoarding vacation days, supporting parents, raising kids—wrestling with the same "money but no time / time but no money" dilemma as everyone else. They may seem to be having the time of their lives on the road, but they've made enormous preparations behind the scenes. **If you want to emulate them, pay attention to what they sacrificed and prepared, and soberly assess how much luck was involved—rather than focusing on their gains.**
